Verdict: The claim as stated doesn't check out
No source documents a "universal credit card" issued by "Affiliated National Hotels" in the 1920s. The claim appears to be a conflation of three real things:
1. Affiliated National Hotels was a real company — but 1930s+, not 1920s, and no card on record.
- Moody family of Galveston, TX; chairman W. L. Moody III. Owned ~32 properties by 1939 (Rice University Cite 77 journal), ~37–40 by the 1950s–60s (Admiral Semmes Mobile, Thomas Jefferson Birmingham, Claypool Indianapolis, Menger San Antonio, Galvez Galveston, etc. — listed in a 1962 Mid-Continent Banker ad via FRASER/St. Louis Fed).
- Conrad Hilton briefly worked for them during the Depression after the Moodys took over his hotels (1931, as the "National Hotel Company").
- Zero evidence it issued any credit/charge card, and no documented footprint in the 1920s.
2. The actual hotel "universal" card was the American Hotel Association's Universal Travelcard — 1953/1956, not 1920s.
- Evans & Schmalensee (Paying with Plastic): "In 1956, the American Hotel Association established the Universal Travelcard... billed cardholders the same $30 fee as Diners Club."
- Court record (Hotel Credit Card Corp. v. American Express, 13 A.D.2d 189): the hotel credit-card system was "using since 1953."
- Bought by American Express in 1958 (160,000 members, 4,500 hotels) and folded into its new card.
3. The "1920s hotel chains + universal credit card" wording traces to Britannica:
"Credit-card use originated in the U.S. in the 1920s; early credit cards were issued by various firms (e.g., oil companies and hotel chains) for use at their outlets only. The first universal credit card, accepted by a variety of establishments, was issued by Diners' Club in 1950."
The adjacent sentences about "1920s hotel chains" and "first universal credit card" likely got scrambled into one claim.

- If someone claims a 1920s "universal credit card from affiliated national hotels," they're almost certainly garbling Britannica's wording + the AHA Universal Travelcard (1950s) + the real Affiliated National Hotels (1930s Moody chain).

- Every authoritative history (Britannica, Mandell's The Credit Card Industry: A History, Evans & Schmalensee, History.com) dates the first universal card to Diners Club, 1950.







# Part 2


Universal (multi-merchant) credit & charge plans introduced before 1945
Verdict: Universal plans did exist before 1945 — but they cluster into three distinct forms, and none of them was a bank/third-party card. The scholarly consensus (Encyclopedia of American Studies, Fed staff, Vanatta) that "the first universal/general-purpose card = Diners Club 1950" survives for cards, but breaks down for credit instruments. The oldest documented universal credit plan I found is Paris, 1856.
Tier 1 — True third-party, multi-merchant, cross-industry credit (the real answers)
1. Dufayel "Abonnements" (Grands Magasins Dufayel / François Crespin) — Paris, 1856
The earliest documented multi-merchant, third-party consumer credit plan — predating Diners Club by 94 years and Provident by 24.
- Mechanics: Customers paid cash toward vouchers ("bons"/"Bezugsscheine") worth ~5× what they'd paid in, i.e., credit was sold as spendable paper. Vouchers were redeemable at listed participating stores — including third-party affiliates like La Samaritaine (which paid Dufayel 18% commission on voucher sales it accepted). 800 collectors in Paris by 1901 collecting weekly home payments; claimed 2M "abonnés."
- Sources: Anaïs Albert, Annales (via Cairn.info): vouchers "worth five times the value of the sum that he had been paid in cash. With these tickets in hand, the holders quickly purchased whatever they desired from any of the stores, lists of which they were given"; Raphael Ruoss, Auf Pump (2023, open access, Wallstein) — explicitly proposes English check traders copied Dufayel's system; Brett Wemp thesis (Dufayel's "eighteen percent cut of all sales at its affiliated stores like La Samaritaine").
- Merchant scope: multi-merchant, cross-retail-category. Universal in the city where it operated.
2. UK "Check Trading" — Provident Clothing & Supply Co., Bradford, 1880 (and imitators)
The most universal pre-1945 credit instrument anywhere: paper "checks" spendable as cash at thousands of unrelated shops.
- Mechanics: Agent sold the customer a check (10s., £1, 30s., £2, up to £20); customer spent it at any participating shop; shop returned checks to the check trader at a discount of 8–20% (this funded the credit); customer repaid check-value + "poundage" (1s. per £1) over ~20 weekly agent visits. Checks were divisible — "retailers were instructed to endorse the expenditure on the back of the check, which could then be taken to other shops until it was exhausted." A "travelling check" variant was expressly designed for spending across several suppliers.
- Scale: 14,000 participating retailers by the 1930s; £18.1M lent by Provident alone in 1939; merchandise "from photographs to bassinettes and from barometers to artificial teeth" (1908 report). UK check+ticket systems were worth £120M p.a. by the late 1930s — 3.5% of all retail sales (Board of Trade). 435 check traders by 1960s (National Federation of Check Traders).
- Pre-1945 imitators: Practical Clothing & Supply Co. (St Helens, 1910); City & Suburban Check Trading Association (Nottingham retailers' coop, 1920s); Cattles (Hull, 1927); L&P Trading and Supply Association (London); John Paget & Son (Leicester).
- Sources: Sean O'Connell & Chris Reid, "Working-class consumer credit in the UK 1880–1939," Economic History Review (2005); McFall & Burton chapter (2016); Dictionary of Business Biography (Waddilove entry: "Trading checks were offered for up to £20 and paid for by weekly instalments").
3. Travelers Hotel Credit Letter (Walz organization, New York) — operating by 1928; roots 1893–94
The US's only true pre-1945 third-party, cross-establishment credit scheme found.
- Lineage: Edgar A. Walz founded the National Hotel Keepers Protective Association (Chicago, 1893 — a debtor blacklist/collective collections network) → National Debtor Record Co. (1894) → Travelers Hotel Credit Letter Service "a few years later" (NYT obituary, Apr 5, 1935) → Hotel Credit Letter Company, 342 Madison Ave.
- Mechanics: Traveling salesmen applied for a Credit Letter; independent hotels nationwide honored it — the surviving 1928 pamphlet is literally "a list of hotels that will honor Travelers Hotel Credit Letters… organized by city and state… An application form is included" (Digital Commonwealth / Historic New England). The credit was backstopped by Walz's debtor-reporting machinery.
- Continuation: successor product named TravelCard (card-format launch date undocumented — flag as unresolved); firm renamed Traveletter Corporation in 1941 (Gelco history, citing Star Tribune, 2003). A 1956 letter shows Walz's successor firm (American Hotel Credit Corp., S.C. Walz) administering the American Hotel Association's Universal Travelcard — the lineage runs straight through the war.
- Merchant scope: cross-hotel (independent operators), third-party-operated. Genuinely "universal" within hospitality.
4. Seattle cooperative charge network — 1930s
City-wide multi-store cooperative charge arrangement: "cooperative networks did emerge in some cities prior to World War II — in Seattle, for example" (Neal Swartz, Big Data & Society, citing Lewis Mandell, The Credit Card Industry: A History, 1990, p.18). Cross-store credit by cooperative arrangement; single city. Secondhand citation — Mandell's book is the place to verify details.
Tier 2 — One credential, many stores — but credit still granted store-by-store
5. Charga-Plate consortiums — Farrington Manufacturing system (1928); consortiums from 1934/36
The closest pre-1945 analog to a "wallet card."
- Mechanics: Farrington plate (Boston, 1928; Filene's first adopter 1929). Notches: circular = city, square = store; one plate could carry several square notches (Virginia Tech SCUA). Consortiums issued shared plates:
- Boston Retail Trade Board common notched plate (Lipartito, U. Mich. MPRA); local-history record of a seven-store Boston multi-store plate scheme in 1934.
- Hartford Charga-Plate Associates — formed by five Hartford department stores, documented from 1936: "One plate may be notched for any one or more or all the member stores. 697,504 plates or notches were provided in the period 1936 to May 31, 1953" — Hartford Charga-Plate Associates v. Youth Centre-Cinderella Stores, 215 F.2d 668 (2d Cir. 1954) (primary federal-court source).
- Also Philadelphia ("Charga-Plate Association Inc."), Providence, Richmond ("Charga-Plate Associates of Richmond"), Cleveland ("big six"), Buffalo (1936), NYC cooperating stores (Bloomingdale's, Arnold Constable, Saks, Franklin Simon, Gimbels).
- Crucial distinction: the shared plate shared identification and bookkeeping only — the Hartford record shows each store furnished its own customer list, and per Lipartito each new store still ran an independent credit investigation. Separate accounts per store; no consolidated billing; no third-party credit. Honored at many stores, but not "one plan."
- Scholarly framing: Evans & Schmalensee (Paying with Plastic): after the war retailers formed "cooperative card operations similar to those begun in the 1930s."

What did NOT exist before 1945 (strong negative evidence)
- No bank-operated multi-merchant charge plan. The definitive study — Sean Vanatta, "Charge Account Banking" (Enterprise & Society, 2018) — opens with Biggins, 1946, and mentions no wartime-or-earlier plan. Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas Circular 68-172 (1968): "Bank participation in charge-card credit plans came rather late in the game." The comprehensive Annals consumer-credit survey (March 1938) covers store charge accounts and bank personal loans — no bank charge plans.
- Boundary case: Charg-It, 1946 (Biggins, Flatbush National Bank) — first bank plan: first trial was Bay Shore, NY with 11 Main Street merchants (Newsday, Aug 16, 1946: "Bank Starts First Credit Plan"); used credit scrip + card; the "two-block Flatbush radius" story is unsubstantiated folklore (Vanatta fn.18). Re-implemented at Paterson Savings & Trust c.1950; Franklin National's card-based "Charge-It" followed May 1952.
- No pre-1945 "central charge" plan (credit-bureau-run centralized billing serving many stores): documented in early 1950s (Crowder, Credit World, 1953; DOJ consent-decree definitions of "central credit service plans" are post-war). Pre-war bureaus shared information (Credit Bureau of Greater NY, 1935), not billing.
- First revolving credit = Bloomingdale's 1938 "permanent budget account" — single merchant.
- Terminology origin: Edward Bellamy, Looking Backward (1887), coined "credit card" — as a universal state card: "a credit card issued him with which he procures at the public storehouses, found in every community, whatever he desires."
Excluded per your scope (but adjacent, for completeness)
- UATP/Air Travel Card 1936 (17 airlines by ~1946) — cross-company, single industry.
- July 1938 oil-card cross-exchange (SOCAL + regional oil cos. honoring each other's cards) — first national cross-company card acceptance; single industry; single hobbyist source (creditcollectibles.com) — verify in TNEC 1939 hearings.
