
# Banking Magazine 1940-08, page 61, 62:

## Buy-O-Matic Plan

The First National Bank and
Trust Company (Freeport, N. Y.) is
the 22nd institution to install the new
Buy-O-Matic finance plan since its introduction in May. Called “the simplest way to buy and pay”, the plan
offers the small merchant a medium
through which he may successfully
compete with the merchandising of
large retail stores in extending consumer credit without charging interest,
or adding to retail prices.


In the operation of the plan the
merchant does the selling job completely.

Customers who want deferred
credit are given a Buy-O-Matic application.

After this has been qualified at
the bank, the customer is invited in to
get his Buy-O-Matic checks. After
signing a six-month note, payable in
monthly instalments, without interest,
he receives the book of checks in
amounts of $60, $90, $120, $150 or
$180, or any combination of these
amounts.

The book contains checks of
various denominations, $1, $2, $5 and
$10. The customer then signs the
checks in the presence of a bank officer—
like a traveler’s check.

When these checks are offered in cash
payment of merchandise, the merchant
validates them and receives face value
credit when they are deposited to his
account at the bank. On the first and
fifteenth of each month, as a merchandising sales commission, the merchant
sends 6 per cent of the value of all
checks spent with him to the Buy-0-Matic headquarters in New York City.

In turn that office sends a check
for 4 per cent of the funds advanced to
the bank. Of this, the bank retains 3
per cent as its share of the merchandising commission and sets up a reserve of
1 per cent for losses against which deductions may be made for customer
notes more than 90 days overdue.

When the reserve has been built up to 10 per
cent of the entire volume, the balance
becomes the property of the Buy-0-
Matic Company.

Among other interesting angles to
the plan is the fact that the customer
becomes liable to the bank only to the
extent that he uses the checks; unused
checks are redeemed in full.

There is a
condition, however, that checks become invalid if not used or redeemed in
six months.

Experience to date shows
that checks are spient within the first
ten days after issuance.

The 3 per cent
accruing to the bank is equivalent to
receiving 6 per cent discount on the
same money, as it is 3 per cent in advance on six months’ discount basis.

To stimulate volume, merchants receive free advertising materials and are
listed in the sectional directory of
Buy-O-Matic merchants which is
widely distributed.

## Additional research and source audit — 17 October 2026

The original notes above are retained. The findings below distinguish contemporary evidence from later recollection. The article section uses the four source labels `buyomatic-nassau-1940`, `buyomatic-banking-1940`, `buyomatic-patterson`, and `buyomatic-regulation-w`.

### Findings

Buy-O-Matic was a bank-financed shopping-cheque plan operating on Long Island by July 1940. A borrower obtained a book of fixed-denomination cheques after credit approval and signing a six-month note. Participating independent merchants accepted the cheques, obtained bank deposit credit, and paid a commission supporting the bank, the plan operator, and a loss reserve. The customer was advertised interest-free credit without a down payment or carrying charge. This is strong evidence of a payment arrangement involving banks, an operator and independent merchants before the postwar card plans, but it does not establish that a physical credit card was issued.

**Date conclusion:** describe a **reported 1939 origin and a documented 1940 rollout**. Patterson's 1963 dissertation dates establishment to 1939, citing a 1957 letter from a participant. The August 1940 contemporary report describes introduction “in May,” naturally read as May 1940 in that article's context, without explicitly printing the year after May. No contemporary 1939 advertisement or exact opening date was located in this research. A development phase followed by commercial rollout is possible, but remains an interpretation, not an established reconciliation.

### S1. Contemporary operating description: _Banking_, August 1940, pp. 61–62

- Article heading: **“Buy-O-Matic Plan.”**
- Publication: _Banking_, vol. 33, no. 2, August 1940, American Bankers Association.
- [Internet Archive issue record](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-bankers-association-aba-banking-journal_1940-08_33_2).
- [Open scan on Wikimedia Commons](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b1/Banking_1940-08-_Vol_33_Iss_2_%28IA_sim_american-bankers-association-aba-banking-journal_1940-08_33_2%29.pdf#page=63).
- Printed pages **61–62** correspond to **PDF pages 63–64** in the Commons scan. Both pages were rendered and visually inspected; the supplied transcription matches the substantive terms.

This is the most detailed contemporary account located. Its opening identifies **First National Bank and Trust Company, Freeport, New York**, as the **22nd institution to install the plan since its introduction in May**. It presents the plan as a way for small retailers to compete with larger stores' credit facilities without adding customer interest or increasing retail prices. That last point is the source's presentation; it is not independent evidence that participating merchants never adjusted prices.

#### Application, spending and repayment

1. A merchant gives the prospective borrower an application.
2. The bank qualifies the application and invites the approved customer to collect cheques.
3. The customer signs a six-month note, repayable monthly without interest.
4. Books have totals of **$60, $90, $120, $150 or $180**, or combinations of these amounts. Thus $180 is not a demonstrated absolute credit ceiling.
5. Individual cheques are for **$1, $2, $5 and $10**. The customer signs them in a bank officer's presence, in the manner of travellers' cheques.
6. A member merchant validates the cheques and deposits them for face-value credit to its bank account.
7. The customer is liable only for used cheques. Unused cheques can be redeemed in full, but must be spent or redeemed within six months.

The report says the experience available at the time indicated cheques were spent within the first ten days after issue. It supplies no sample size, loan volume, default rate or audited financial result. This should not become a claim about every borrower or the plan's proven profitability.

#### Merchant commission and loss reserve

The merchant sends headquarters **6% of the value of cheques spent at the shop**, on the **first and fifteenth of each month**. Headquarters then sends the bank an amount equal to **4% of funds advanced**. The bank retains **3%** and puts **1%** in a reserve for losses. Notes more than **90 days overdue** can be charged against that reserve.

For a simple $100 illustration, the implied allocation is $3 bank revenue, $1 reserve and $2 retained by the operator. The $2 is arithmetic from the reported split, not a separately itemised figure in the article. Likewise, it is the operator's share before its expenses, not a documented net profit.

The report says that when the reserve reaches **10% of the entire volume**, the balance becomes the Buy-O-Matic Company's property. The wording does not define the volume period or provide the underlying agreement. Do not invent a precise reserve formula, government guarantee, unlimited indemnity, or final allocation of losses beyond the reserve.

The magazine equates the bank's upfront 3% on a six-month basis to a 6% discount return on the same money. **This is not a modern APR** and does not account for a declining instalment balance. It also does not make 3% a customer interest charge: the merchant funds it.

Merchants receive advertising material and inclusion in a sectional merchant directory. The company has headquarters in **New York City**, but this item gives no street address, incorporation date, officer list or identification of its very first participating bank.

### S3. Retrospective account: Harlan R. Patterson, 1963

- Full title: _A Study of Charge Account Banking and Its Financial Performance_.
- Author: **Harlan R. Patterson**; Ph.D. dissertation, Michigan State University, **1963**. The opening approval page is dated **3 May 1963**.
- Local source: `books_papers/A STUDY OF CHARGE ACCOUNT BANKING 1963.pdf`.
- Relevant section: **“The Origin of Bank-Operated Charge Plans,” printed pp. 14–15, PDF pp. 29–30**. Both relevant pages were rendered and visually inspected.

Patterson dates the New York-area plan's establishment to **1939**, by **R. A. Dousseau and associates**. He reports **21 banks and close to 7,000 merchants by 1941**, and attributes the plan's halt to Regulation W together with U.S. entry into World War II.

His footnote 21 identifies the historical source as a **personal letter of 5 December 1957**, from **R. A. Dousseau, president of Check Master Inc., New York**, to **James H. C. Duncan, First National Bank and Trust Company of Kalamazoo, Michigan**. The original letter was **not** located or inspected. Patterson is therefore an accessible secondary account drawing on a participant's later recollection, rather than a contemporary launch announcement.

The 21-bank figure conflicts with S1's description of a 22nd adopter in August 1940. It could reflect different definitions, attrition or an error, but there is no evidence here to choose among these explanations. Prefer the contemporary **22nd adopter** wording for the 1940 rollout, and attribute the later **nearly 7,000 merchants** figure to Patterson. Do not silently combine these into a verified continuous growth series.

Patterson does not give a precise closure date, termination notice, borrower count, turnover, loss figures, or a description of a card. His following paragraphs concern **Charg-It**, and their revolving-credit and 8% merchant-discount terms must not be imported into Buy-O-Matic.

### S4. Contemporary regulatory context: Federal Reserve, September 1941

- [Federal Reserve Bulletin, September 1941, full text at FRASER](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/title/federal-reserve-bulletin-62/september-1941-20991/fulltext).
- Relevant passages: **pp. 825–826**, explanation of the purpose and initial effective date; **pp. 839–845**, Regulation W; **pp. 862–863**, explanatory questions and answers.

Regulation W was adopted on **21 August 1941** and began taking effect on **1 September 1941**, with some provisions phased in later. It restricted consumer instalment credit in a defence economy. The explanation connects the measure to inflation, civilian demand, and materials required for defence. It did **not** prohibit all consumer lending.

The initial terms included required down payments for specified durable goods and an **18-month maximum maturity** for covered instalment credit. The questions and answers describe a **20%** down payment on refrigerators, vacuum cleaners, washing machines and certain other appliances. Buy-O-Matic's six-month maturity was already shorter than that general maximum; the original zero-down offer, and any restrictions on how the loans could finance specified goods, are more relevant than an assertion that its loans were too long.

This source verifies the regulatory change and its purpose. It does **not** name Buy-O-Matic or independently demonstrate the company's closure. That causal claim comes from S3. Avoid claiming that the company was outlawed on 1 September, or that its first bank stopped issuing cheques on a known date.

### Other leads and exclusions

- A secondary transcription points to _Printers' Ink_, **24 October 1941**, vol. 197, **p. 88**, joking about the vogue for product names ending in “matic,” including the Buy-O-Matic loan plan. [Discovery lead](https://gadgetry.wythoff.net/tags/need). The original issue could not be retrieved (HTTP 503), so the anecdote is **not included in the article**. Even if confirmed, a passing joke would not establish that the plan was still issuing credit on that exact date.
- Hollywood Federal Savings and Loan's **Buy-O-Matic**, introduced in **May 1977**, is a later point-of-sale debit service described in [_Datamation_, January 1979, p. 77](https://www.bitsavers.org/magazines/Datamation/197901.pdf). It is a namesake and should not be folded into the 1939/1940 plan's history without evidence of continuity.
- General web searches for the historical name are heavily contaminated by a modern cryptocurrency and by unrelated “-matic” products. No claims about those products belong in this section.

### Archive coverage and remaining questions

In addition to the three starting sources, the research retrieved and searched Internet Archive OCR for _Banking_: **June 1939; May, June, July, September and October 1940; January and June 1941**. Searches used variants of Buy-O-Matic, including zero-for-O and spacing variations, and Dousseau. These checks yielded no additional direct references. This is a targeted set of issues, not an exhaustive search of all banking literature, and negative OCR results do not prove absence from a scan.

The strongest next archival target is **Dousseau's 5 December 1957 letter**, through Patterson's research papers or Duncan's/Kalamazoo bank records. A contemporary announcement from **1939 or May 1940**, an original cheque specimen or merchant agreement, and a **1941–1942 termination notice** would address the main gaps. Until then, keep the exact first bank, launch day, borrower numbers, financial performance, cross-bank acceptance rules, reserve accounting details, and final closure date open.

### Article decisions

- Lead with the documented shopping experience and merchant network, in the direct narrative style of the Diners Club in India section.
- Explain the 1939/1940 distinction once and retain source-specific dates in the footnotes.
- Use the contemporary newspaper illustration, with its exact date and page, rather than a reconstructed card.
- Treat the $120 book / $20 monthly repayment example and the $100 fee allocation as arithmetic illustrations.
- Describe the cheques as borrowed purchasing power. Do not call them prepaid coupons funded from the customer's own deposits, or assert a reusable card/revolving account.
- Preserve the distinction between repayment of the consumer's six-month note and the merchant's twice-monthly commission remittances.
- Attribute the 7,000-merchant estimate and wartime ending to Patterson, and record the conflicting bank counts.
- Adjust the immediately preceding cartoon discussion so it no longer claims bank shopping credit was wholly untried in the early 1940s. Buy-O-Matic itself disproves that formulation.
