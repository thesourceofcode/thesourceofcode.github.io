# Department stores: research and rewrite sources

Research checked 17 October 2026 for the Department Stores section of `_posts/2026-05-15-payments-101-credit-card-chronology.md`. The Diners Club in India section supplied the writing model: dated events, named people and establishments, concrete transactions, advertisements and source notes.

**Expanded PDF pass:** Following the request for deeper book research, the current `books_papers` collection was inventoried again: **48 PDFs**, including four titles absent from the earlier text-extraction index. Broader searches and substantial new reading are documented below under “Deeper book research.” The accompanying `department-stores-pdf-search-index.csv` lists search-hit pages for every current PDF. A page hit is a lead, not a declaration that the whole work was read.

## Scope and method

The starting collection, `books_papers/newspapers/department-stores/`, contains **16 files: seven PDFs and nine raster images**. Two ProQuest PDFs reproduce the same article, so they are not independent corroboration. One screenshot lacks a date and publication identification. All 16 were extracted or OCRed and inspected; full newspaper pages were rendered to check the relevant advertisements, and the image-only page in the ProQuest export was read visually rather than relying on its database abstract.

The existing post images, including the full Wanamaker launch advertisement and Morgan's September 1935 detail, were also checked. The relevant front matter and existing galleries were preserved.

For wider context, searchable text from **89 local PDFs** across `books_papers`, `bank_credit_cards`, `oil_credit_cards` and the research folders was screened for Charga-Plate/charge plate, Henry Morgan, budgetted charge, revolving charge/credit and Retail Service Bureau. This was a relevance screen, not a claim that every page of every book was read. The substantive passages read are listed below. Sparse or image-only text was not treated as proof that a document contained nothing relevant. No new online research was needed for this rewrite; the user's unverified web leads in `research/store-accounts-research.md` remain available for later work.

## Decisions carried into the rewrite

1. **Separate the credential from the credit terms.** A Charga-Plate could identify either a regular charge account or a revolving one. Neither the metal nor the embossing by itself determined when a customer paid.
2. **Avoid invention mythology.** Use late-1920s plate development; B.C.A. advertising by September 1935; Wanamaker's public launch on 22 August 1938. None establishes a worldwide first revolving account. Morgan's explicitly unnamed rival stays unnamed in that passage.
3. **Distinguish sharing identification from pooling finance.** Boston's seven stores required existing or separately approved accounts. A shared plate does not establish a shared lender, bill or credit limit.
4. **Describe the mechanism the sources support.** Embossed details, an imprinter and carbon-paper forms. Drop the blanket inked-ribbon account and do not turn one store's notch pattern into a universal city/store shape rule.
5. **Keep the deposit and limits visible.** Morgan's requires an initial deposit; Wanamaker's October example has an annual spending cap. Early revolving plans were not automatically today's minimum-payment contracts.
6. **Separate replenishment from finance charges.** Wanamaker, Rhodes, Rosenbaum's and FitzGerald's advertise no carrying charge; Gimbels in 1949 expressly adds one. Do not generalize either feature to every store or era.
7. **Correct the wartime story.** Restrictions complicated credit, but mechanical account systems helped stores comply and spread during the war. Allen's plates were retained by the store; the Hale's shopper carried a plate.
8. **Attribute promotional claims.** Speed, privacy, security, years of prior use, household discipline and priority are advertisements' promises, not automatic proof of outcomes. The annual-budget customer is expressly an advertising example.
9. **Keep modern comparisons modest.** Retain the connection to signature panels and manual imprinting. Remove time-sensitive claims about what the latest cards carry or whether paper slips are obsolete.

## Deeper book research

### Search coverage and OCR

The first pass was too concentrated on newspaper examples and a small set of book passages. This pass extends the question from the plate itself to the store account around it: approval, identification, authorization, billing, solicitation, partial payment, limits and financing.

All **48 current PDFs in `books_papers`**, recursively including newspaper PDFs, were checked for the following groups of terms:

- `charge account` / `charge accounts`, allowing line breaks and hyphens;
- `open book`, `open-book`, `open account`;
- `charge plate`, `Charga-Plate`, `credit plate`;
- budget/budgetted, revolving, option and permanent accounts or credit;
- Addressograph, Farrington, imprint, emboss, charge check and sales slip.

Line-end hyphenation was normalized before matching. Contents, indexes and nearby passages supplied additional terms and routes through the material, including charge transactions, charge-takes, charge phones, identification, coins, reference clearance, limits/overlimits and business-building. The CSV records candidate pages from the broad term groups, not every page located by subsequent reading. The page locators are one-based **PDF pages**; printed-page references in the notes below are separate.

Four current files were newly extracted: *The Retail Charge Account*, *Credit Management*, Leffingwell's manual and Corbion's salesmanship manual. The last had **393 PDF pages but only about 3,400 extracted characters**, almost entirely Google's cover notice. Treating its empty term results as meaningful would miss the book's charge-transaction instructions. Every page was therefore rendered and OCRed at a maximum dimension of 2,100 pixels. The resulting **424,513 characters** were searched again; passages used in the post were checked against the actual page images. Searchable text is preserved in `books_papers/the_principles_of_salesmanship_1907-ocr.txt`, with method, source hash and limitations in the adjacent `-ocr-source.json`. The original PDF is unchanged. The index reports the new OCR text size for this book.

*The Origins of Value* remains a separate text-coverage limitation: its extracted layer is essentially empty and its broad historical subject does not make it a priority source for department-store operations. The five image-only Diners Club newspaper PDFs concern later travel-card operations. Their empty search results do not establish the absence of relevant words. No completeness claim is made for OCR-free images in those files.

### Frederick W. Walter, ed., The Retail Charge Account (1922)

`books_papers/The_retail_charge_account.pdf`; prepared under the direction of the Educational Committee of the Associated Retail Credit Men of New York City, Inc.; Ronald Press. Walter is identified in the contributor list as Gimbel Brothers' credit manager. The preface dates the underlying lectures and discussions to **1919–1920 and 1920–1921** and identifies a membership of over 600 retailers and bankers. The manual represents the credit profession's prescriptions, not a representative survey of all stores.

Substantive reading covered **printed pp. 3–8, 17–32, 53–79, 154–160, 207–218 and 251–258** (PDF pp. 23–28, 37–52, 73–99, 174–180, 227–238 and 271–278), plus the preface, contributor list, contents and illustration list. Important passages:

### William Amelius Corbion, The Principles of Salesmanship, Deportment and System (1907)

`books_papers/the_principles_of_salesmanship_1907.pdf`, George W. Jacobs & Co.; copyright page says **published November 1907**. Read preface/contents and printed **pp. 228–234 and 307–318** (PDF pp. 235–241 and 314–325) after full-book OCR. Checked the key quote and $10 rule in page images.

- **P. 312:** Identification proves the customer is herself and “does not give authority to deliver goods.” Charges must be approved by the credit department, a representative or reference to the inspector's coin book. The explicit distinction is much stronger than treating the token as automatic authorization.
- **Pp. 312–313:** Delivery to the account address and delivery elsewhere have different identification requirements; baggage-master shipments are an exception even when the nominal address appears unchanged. The rewrite does not need all these rules, but they constrain generalization.
- **Pp. 313–314:** Description of numbered metal charge coin, instruction to transcribe its number only after seeing it; spurious coins or another store's coin could be presented. Orders and sub-orders are separate purchasing authorities.
- **Pp. 316–317:** For take-home purchases, a coin identifies up to $10 under the described procedure; larger purchases or missing credentials go to the aisle manager. A coin marked “refer” travels with the check to the credit bureau by tube or messenger. This is a local/procedural threshold, not a nationwide maximum or a card credit limit.
- **Pp. 228–232:** Catalogues clerical mistakes including the wrong charge name/initial or omission of the coin number, and their consequences for tracing and billing. Reinforces the subsequent demand for accurate copying.

These pages contain no evidence of an embossed consumer charge plate in 1907. The discovery strengthens the account/authorization context and the transition out of coins; it does not move the Charga-Plate launch back to 1907.

### Emery E. Olson and J. W. Hallman, Credit Management (1925)

`books_papers/credit_management_1925.pdf`, Ronald Press. The volume's title and copyright pages identify the authors and date. Read printed **pp. 36–39, 341–348, 356–359 and 363**, with nearby reference lists; searched broader credit/retail and open-account hits.

The most useful chapter is **XXVI, “Special Features of Retail Credit.”** It describes personal interviews, applications, monthly settlement expectations, family authorization, reference checks, credit limits and identification. Pp. **345–346** distinguish charge coins/courtesy cards, floor-manager recognition and signatures, saying none is fraud-proof. Signature comparison against visible files could substitute for a forgotten coin; the text says few stores then required a signature on **all** charge purchases. Do not universalize the take-home signature requirement into a rule for every sale.

P. **38** treats the detailed sales slip, usually recording an identification coin number and delivery checks, as evidence beyond a book entry. The chapter's praise of interviewers' “sixth sense” exposes a subjective decision process; it is not proof of reliable prediction. Repeated references lead back to Walter's manual, helping connect these professional accounts rather than count them as wholly independent evidence.

### William Henry Leffingwell, ed., The Office Appliance Manual (1926)

`books_papers/leffingwell-1926-office-appliance-manual-ocr.pdf`; its provenance JSON identifies the HathiTrust source and added cover. Printed pagination is **31 below PDF pagination** in the relevant part of the book. Read the **pp. 405–445** addressing-machine discussion (PDF pp. 436–476), including illustrations, and relevant credit-register material on **pp. 308–311** (PDF pp. 339–342). Additional mechanism hits were screened to distinguish check-writing/perforating machinery from address-plate equipment.

Directly useful passages and figures:

- **Pp. 407–414 / figs. 163–168:** plates, filing forms, embossing equipment; fig. 168 (p. 414) shows a standard keyboard connected to punches and dies that emboss metal. This is the new illustration in the restored embossing subsection.
- **Pp. 421–425:** metal, stencil and type-slug printing systems are distinct. Metal plates can print by ribbon, ribbon offset or direct ink; plates are one component of an apparatus including embossers, printers and filing equipment. This does not license a single printing explanation for every later customer imprinter.
- **Pp. 422–423:** materials and different embossing operations; corrections could be re-embossed over existing characters. The rewrite uses the machinery description, without assuming every Charga-Plate could be edited in precisely this way.
- **Pp. 437–445:** addressing equipment supports marketing, records, shipments and collections. Pp. 439–440 explain marketing data kept alongside an address; pp. 444–445 discuss statements and collections. The chapter continues beyond p. 437; the range cited by Wolters is a selection, not the entire chapter.
- **Pp. 308–311:** a credit register organizes sales slips and exposes earlier balances so staff can check account status. This provides context for bookkeeping behind the credential, but is not claimed as a Charga-Plate system.

The volume is sponsored by office-appliance manufacturers. Its speed, cost-saving and near-infallibility language must be read as industry claims. It documents established office technology before the late-1920s Charga-Plate rollout; it does **not** prove consumer Charga-Plates existed in 1926.

### Rolf Nugent, Consumer Credit and Economic Stability (1939)

`books_papers/Consumer-Credit-Economic-Stability.pdf`, Russell Sage Foundation. Read the relevant open-book discussions on **pp. 83–86, 100–103, 123, 126–128, 137 and 139**, plus earlier historical context.

P. **84** says quality stores competed with installment sellers by tacitly accepting partial payments; some used “budget plan” terminology to avoid the stigma of “installment.” P. **101** describes the 1920s extension of charge-account selling and merchants' reluctance to press desirable customers who might take their business to more accommodating rivals. This contemporary analysis is a substantial corrective to a rigid story in which nobody could carry a charge balance before a celebrated revolving-credit launch.

The rewrite distinguishes **tolerated partial repayment** from a **standing, agreed promise to replenish buying power as payments arrive**. Nugent's broader statistical treatment concerns multiple types of open-book merchant and service creditor, so it is not reduced to department stores alone. No new “first revolving account” is inferred from it.

### Deeper reading in Hyman, Mandell and other histories

- **Hyman, Debtor Nation:** the expanded pass read regulatory passages on **pp. 99, 103, 105, 107–108, 111 and 113–116**, the continuation on **pp. 127–130**, and the postwar discussion on **pp. 145–160**, in addition to the first pass's pp. 117–126. Pp. 113–116 describe actual installment/charge hybrids and the later extension of controls. Pp. 127–129 show that the Korean War regulatory episode differed from WWII; a statement about one wartime regime should not be applied indiscriminately to the other. Pp. **149–155** connect store expansion, repeat purchases and flexible limits; pp. **156–160** explain option accounts, finance-charge income and growing demands on stores' capital. The new closing subsection draws on those latter passages. The quotation about becoming one's own credit manager is checked on **p. 157** and is **quoted by Hyman**, not from a separately inspected original interview. Some of Hyman's aggregate dollar figures on p. 160 do not add up internally; they are not reproduced.
- **Mandell, The Credit Card Industry:** **pp. 23–25** read in full. P. 25 supplies store-specific notching in the 1948 New York group and an optional thirty-day/no-finance-charge plan at J. L. Hudson in **1956**. Hyman emphasizes broader option-account change beginning in **1958**. The rewrite avoids a single invention date. Mandell's p. 24 priority framing around Wanamaker and Bamberger is qualified by the actual 1935/1938 advertisements and WWD report already documented above.
- **Remus, Credit and Coverture:** expanded reading of **pp. 6–14**. Important for the shift from personal book credit to large-scale account administration, biased access, and variations in married women's property/liability rules. The post's aside rests on the directly inspected 1922 manual; Remus supplies interpretation and a warning against universal legal claims. No present-day legal guidance is being offered.
- **Hillel Black, Buy Now, Pay Later:** further **pp. 97–101** read. Includes consumer-finance education and teenage charge programs. Useful later-context material, but not inserted into this already substantial section: some illustrations are anonymous, the book is explicitly polemical and its fine-grained rate arguments need separate handling.
