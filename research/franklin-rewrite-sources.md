## Sources used

- “A Bank's Retail Charge Account Service,” *Banking*, June 1952, p. 122, supplied as `bank_credit_cards/franklin_charge_service.jpg`. Read visually. Merchant conference and Roth's “We'll try”; 23,000 families; more than $1 million in charges; reported sales gains; standard 5 percent discount; immediate, nonrecourse merchant payment; monthly customer billing; dedicated credit department and ledger processing. Retained the existing card illustration from this page; no new portrait or image added.
- “Nassau's Shoppers to Get Bank Credit,” *New York Times*, 1 May 1952, p. 31, supplied as `bank_credit_cards/franklin national bank launch 1952.pdf`. Read the rendered scan because OCR extraction has only a database header. Roth announced the plan the previous day; prior pilot; advertisements seeking merchants beginning May 1; no scrip; merchant-originated customer enrollment; bank credit decisions; sale authorization limits and telephone checks.
- Federal Reserve System Task Group, *Bank Credit-Card and Check-Credit Plans*, July 1968; local `bank_credit_cards/bank_credit_card_report.pdf`, a copy distributed with Dallas Circular 68-172 of 24 September 1968. Printed p. 9: August 1951 beginning and April 1952 full-scale operation, industry entry/withdrawal and cost problems. Appendix A, printed p. 79 (PDF p. 85): Franklin Charge Plan remains listed in the survey, alongside separate Steady-Credit and Amex Executive Credit plans. [FRASER copy](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/historical/frbdal/circulars/frbdallas_circ_19680924_no68-172.pdf).
- Sean H. Vanatta, *Plastic Capitalism* (2024), ch. 2, pp. 39–48. Merchant-focused origin, Donohue, enrollment, household credit in husbands' names, payment in full, merchant discounts, longer fixed payment periods and retailer objections.
- Vanatta, “Charge Account Banking: A Study of Financial Innovation in the 1950s,” *Enterprise & Society* 19(2) (2018), pp. 352–390; accepted manuscript `books_papers/charge_account_banking.pdf`, internal pp. 12–14, 17–18 and 25–27. Adds the contested 1953 Boyle litigation. The manuscript is labeled as potentially differing from the published version; manuscript page references are identified as such.
- Stearns, *Electronic Value Exchange* (2011), pp. 18–19 and note 67: explicit caution against equating the original Franklin charge plan with modern revolving credit, early-bank scale problems and conflicting accounts of later revolving terms.
- Mandell, *The Credit Card Industry* (1990), pp. 29–30: early bank entrants and larger banks' reluctance.
- “The Evolution of the Debit Card as a Means of Payment,” *Congressional Record—Senate*, 1 February 1978; supplied `bank_credit_cards/congressional_record_franklin_charge_card.pdf`: signed sales-slip drafts replaced scrip and informed later card arrangements.
- Grossman, *American Express* (1987), pp. 278–281: Roth offered Amex promotion/sales versus Franklin credit/bookkeeping in 1957; Amex kept its accounting inside the company.
- Evans/Schmalensee, *Paying with Plastic*, pp. 55–56, consulted for local shopper-card context.
- John Steele Gordon, [“How Credit Cards Fueled Global Economic Growth,” ABA Banking Journal, 31 August 2017](https://bankingjournal.aba.com/2017/08/how-credit-cards-fueled-global-economic-growth/): Boyle credit, fuel-oil origin, 750 merchants / 28,000 customers / $2.5 million first-year charges, Kalamazoo licensing and 30 merchants / 18,000 customers in three weeks. Those totals are attributed retrospective figures, kept separate from the June 1952 contemporary snapshot.

## Main corrections and qualifications

1. Do not describe Franklin as the first bank card without qualification: the article already covers preceding Brooklyn and Paterson experiments.
2. Distinguish August 1951 beginnings, April 1952 full-scale operation, and Roth's public announcement on April 30 as reported May 1. These describe different stages.
3. The original charge arrangement required monthly payment in full, with costs recovered from merchants. Some retrospective sources assign revolving credit to Franklin from the outset; Stearns specifically flags the ambiguity. The text prioritizes contemporary mechanics and Vanatta's detailed reconstruction and does not assert a precise later revolving introduction date.
4. Five percent applied to standard charges; Vanatta records six percent for 60-day charges. Longer fixed terms are not equivalent to indefinite revolving balances.
5. “Without recourse” concerns ordinary customer nonpayment, not exemption for merchant fraud or breaches of the merchant agreement.
6. The June 1952 counts and later ABA totals are attributed separately; reported increases in merchant sales are not presented as an independent causal estimate.
7. Boyle's competing business and Franklin's lawsuit are described as a contested dispute, not an established theft.
8. Many early bank plans failed. The Federal Reserve's September 1967 survey still lists Franklin Charge Plan, so the unsupported early-shutdown claim was removed. The listing does not prove that terms and administration remained unchanged throughout.
9. Removed the unsupported “still extant” claim about basement rooms and the vault. No current building survey was supplied or undertaken.
10. Removed the unsourced claim that the Gourmet/Esquire restaurant program failed for nonpayment. Grossman's documented 1957 offer to Amex provides the relevant later connection without mixing separate programs' outcomes.
